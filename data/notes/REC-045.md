Canti di Prigionia ("Songs of Imprisonment", 1938–41) is a choral triptych by the Istrian-born Italian composer Luigi Dallapiccola, scored for mixed chorus with the strikingly percussive ensemble of two pianos, two harps and percussion. It is one of the first overt musical protests written inside Fascist Europe: Dallapiccola began sketching it in September 1938, days after Mussolini's radio speech announcing Italy's antisemitic racial laws. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canti_di_prigionia), [2](https://americansymphony.org/concert-notes/canti-di-prigionia/)]

### ✊ A protest set in motion by the racial laws

The racial laws were not abstract politics for Dallapiccola — his wife Laura Luzzatto was Jewish, and the campaign threatened her directly. He later testified that the Canti were his answer to the 1938 laws: "Only by means of music would I be able to express my indignation." [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canti_di_prigionia), [2](https://primolevicenter.org/printed-matter/luigi-dallapiccolas-musical-innovations/)] Rather than write agitprop, he chose three historical prisoners awaiting death under state power:

* **Preghiera di Maria Stuarda** — a prayer of Mary, Queen of Scots.
* **Invocazione di Boezio** — from Book Three of Boethius' *Consolation of Philosophy*, written in a Roman prison.
* **Congedo di Girolamo Savonarola** — the farewell from Savonarola's unfinished meditation on the psalm "My hope is in Thee, O Lord". [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canti_di_prigionia)]

### 🎼 Dodecaphony wrapped around the Dies Irae

The work is an early Italian engagement with Schoenberg's twelve-tone method — but its most radical gesture is grafting a row onto medieval plainchant. A twelve-note series underpins the whole triptych, while the 13th-century **Dies Irae** ("Day of Wrath") chant from the Requiem mass threads through all three songs — announced at the outset in harps and timpani, with the tone rows, in one commentator's image, wreathing "themselves around the chant like ivy". The chant anchors the dodecaphony in tonal memory and turns the liturgy of judgment into a political emblem: a day of wrath promised to the persecutors. [[1](https://americansymphony.org/concert-notes/canti-di-prigionia/), [2](https://www.percussionsdestrasbourg.com/en/soixante-ans/dallapiccola-luigi-canti-di-prigionia-2/)]

### 📻 Premieres under the shadow of war

The first song was premiered on Brussels Radio in 1940, weeks before the Nazi invasion of Belgium; the complete triptych was first heard in Rome in December 1941 — by grim coincidence, as Italy's war widened. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canti_di_prigionia)]

### 🧬 Legacy

The Canti opened a lifelong "prisoner" thread in Dallapiccola's work, continued in the opera *Il prigioniero* and *Canti di liberazione*, and established him as the leading Italian dodecaphonist — proof that serial technique could carry moral and political weight, not just constructive rigor.
