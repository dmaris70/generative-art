Voyager is George Lewis's "virtual orchestra" — software that improvises, as an independent musical personality, alongside human players. First premiered in 1987 and revised for decades, it is probably the most-performed work by any AACM composer, and the piece that put "human-computer interaction" into histories of experimental music. The Avant CD documents it definitively: recorded 6 February 1993 in Hertz Hall, University of California at Berkeley, with Lewis on trombone and Roscoe Mitchell on saxophones in duos with the program. [[1](https://paulsteinbeck.com/Paul%20Steinbeck_2019_George%20Lewis's%20Voyager.pdf), [2](https://www.discogs.com/release/840882-George-E-Lewis-Voyager)]

### ⚡ How the software listens

Voyager's music is generated by a routine called *setphrasebehavior*, which every few seconds assembles the virtual orchestra's instruments into "ensembles," each with its own pitch sets, tuning systems, melody-generating algorithms, tempo and density — and decides whether each ensemble will imitate, directly oppose, or ignore the human player. While the musician plays, the software converts sound into MIDI data and tracks some thirty musical parameters; crucially, it does *not* store motives for later development — a technique Lewis considered "essentially Eurocentric" — instead analyzing the player's input as contributions to an evolving sonic environment. [[1](https://paulsteinbeck.com/Paul%20Steinbeck_2019_George%20Lewis's%20Voyager.pdf)]

### 🥁 An AACM aesthetic in code

Lewis, an AACM member since 1971 and its historian, built the Association's practices into the program. Voyager's instrumentation mixes symphonic strings and winds with instruments from Africa, the Americas, and Asia, and its shifting unconventional "ensembles" deliberately evoke AACM multi-instrumentalism — the Art Ensemble of Chicago's "thousand instruments on stage." The design is non-hierarchical: no player, human or machine, is in charge. Lewis calls the system "incarnate," not prosthetic — it makes independent decisions and can perform entire concerts alone; the human's only required input is "start playing" and "stop playing." [[1](https://paulsteinbeck.com/Paul%20Steinbeck_2019_George%20Lewis's%20Voyager.pdf)]

### 🎺 From KIM-1 to Voyager

The lineage runs through Lewis's late-70s encounter with David Behrman's microcomputer music, his first interactive piece *The KIM and I* (The Kitchen, 1979), and *Rainbow Family* (IRCAM, 1984), where Apple II-driven synthesizers improvised with Derek Bailey, Douglas Ewart, Steve Lacy and Joëlle Léandre. Voyager itself took shape at STEIM in Amsterdam from 1985, written in Forth and later recoded in Max/MSP to drive a Yamaha Disklavier. [[1](https://paulsteinbeck.com/Paul%20Steinbeck_2019_George%20Lewis's%20Voyager.pdf)]

### 🧬 Legacy

Performed in hundreds of concerts worldwide, Voyager reframed "artificial intelligence" as a question about culture and dialogue rather than control — "improvisation becomes a negotiation," Lewis says, "rather than just be in control" — decades before the current wave of musical AI. Lewis went on to a MacArthur Fellowship and a Columbia professorship. [[1](https://paulsteinbeck.com/Paul%20Steinbeck_2019_George%20Lewis's%20Voyager.pdf), [2](https://music.columbia.edu/bios/george-e-lewis)]
