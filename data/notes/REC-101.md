In 1961, at Bell Telephone Laboratories in Murray Hill, New Jersey, an IBM 7094 mainframe became the first computer to sing: a rendition of Harry Dacre's 1892 song "Daisy Bell (Bicycle Built for Two)," with synthesized vocals and computer-generated accompaniment. It is the earliest known recording of computer speech synthesis performing a song, and — via Arthur C. Clarke — the direct ancestor of one of cinema's most famous death scenes. [[1](https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?entryid=4445), [2](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/454935-first-song-performed-using-computer-speech-synthesis)]

### ⚡ How the machine sang

* The vocal synthesis was programmed by physicist **John L. Kelly Jr.** with **Carol Lockbaum**, using Kelly's speech-synthesis work at Bell Labs.
* The musical accompaniment was programmed by **Max Mathews**, the father of computer music, whose MUSIC software had made the IBM mainframe a general-purpose sound synthesizer.
* Nothing happened in real time: the computer's output had to be computed, recorded, and transferred to tape before anyone could hear the result. [[1](https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?entryid=4445), [2](https://www.loc.gov/static/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/documents/DaisyBell.pdf)]

### 🎙 A note on attribution

This catalogue lists the work under Max Mathews and John R. Pierce. That is a common shorthand, but slightly imprecise: Pierce — the engineer who coined the word "transistor" and led the Bell Labs research division — was the project's patron and host rather than its programmer; the singing itself is credited to Kelly, Lockbaum and Mathews. Sources also disagree on the machine (IBM 704 vs. 7094 appear in different accounts; the 7094 is the more commonly cited). [[1](https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?entryid=4445), [2](https://www.aneddoticamagazine.com/ibm-704-speech-synthesis/)]

### 🤖 The HAL legacy

Arthur C. Clarke heard a demonstration of the singing computer while visiting his friend Pierce at Bell Labs. He was impressed enough to write it into 2001: A Space Odyssey (novel and film, 1968): as astronaut Dave Bowman disconnects HAL 9000's higher functions, the dying computer regresses to the first thing it was taught — and sings "Daisy Bell," slower and lower, until it stops. A 17-second demo became a permanent cultural image of machine mortality. [[1](https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?entryid=4445)]

### 🧬 Recognition

The recording was added to the Library of Congress **National Recording Registry** in 2009 as a landmark of digital sound, and Guinness World Records lists it as the first song performed using computer speech synthesis.
