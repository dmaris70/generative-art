A Sound Map of the Danube is Annea Lockwood's aural tracing of an entire river: 59 recording sites along the Danube's roughly 2,850-kilometre course from the Black Forest springs to the Black Sea delta, interleaved with the voices of 13 people who live and work on it. Completed in 2005 and premiered that year at the Donaufestival in Krems, Austria, it was issued by Lovely Music as a 3-CD set — 167 minutes of river. [[1](https://www.annealockwood.com/compositions/a-sound-map-of-the-danube/), [2](http://www.lovely.com/titles/cd2083.html)]

### 🌍 Five journeys, nine countries

Between the winter of 2001 and the summer of 2004 Lockwood made five field-recording trips, moving slowly downstream through Germany, Austria, Slovakia, Hungary, Croatia, Serbia, Bulgaria and Romania. She recorded the water's surface and — with hydrophones — its underside: aquatic insects, sediment hiss, the churn of current, plus birds, bells, ferries and weather along the banks [[1](http://www.lovely.com/titles/cd2083.html), [2](https://www.soundohm.com/product/a-sound-map-of-the-danube)].

### 🗺️ Listening with a map

The set includes a fold-out map of the river made by cartographer Baker Vail (Small Blue Maps) with designer Susan Huyser, keyed so the listener can locate each numbered site geographically while listening [[1](http://www.lovely.com/titles/cd2083.html)]. The piece is thus literally a map in sound — a survey with the rigour of cartography and the patience of a naturalist — and also exists as a gallery installation.

### 🎧 Rivers as instruments

Lockwood (b. 1939, New Zealand) has been recording rivers since the 1960s; her Sound Map of the Hudson River (1982) established the form, and a Sound Map of the Housatonic followed in 2010. Her conviction is that a river is not scenery but a sounding body with as much interior differentiation as any orchestra — hydrophones reveal textures no bankside listener suspects [[1](https://www.annealockwood.com/compositions/a-sound-map-of-the-danube/)]. The human interviews — fishermen, a hydrologist, villagers, in their own languages — thread memory and politics through the water sounds, so the map documents a watershed's culture as well as its acoustics.

### 🧬 Significance

Released commercially in 2008, the Danube map is widely regarded as a summit of field-recording-as-composition, praised for making nearly three hours of unprocessed environmental sound function as sustained musical form [[1](https://www.squidco.com/cgi-bin/news/newsView.cgi?newsID=635), [2](http://www.tokafi.com/news/cd-feature-annea-lockwood-sound-map-danube/)]. It has become a touchstone for the ecological turn in sound art: composition as attention, the composer as listener who arranges the world's own material rather than overwriting it.
