It's Gonna Rain (1965) is Steve Reich's first major work and the piece in which he discovered tape phasing — the process that would generate minimalism's entire rhythmic language. Roughly eighteen minutes of music are conjured from a few seconds of a Pentecostal street preacher's voice, looped against itself until speech dissolves into pattern. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It's_Gonna_Rain), [2](https://www.npr.org/sections/deceptivecadence/2015/01/27/381575433/fifty-years-of-steve-reichs-its-gonna-rain)]

### 📻 Brother Walter in Union Square

In 1964 Reich recorded the preacher Brother Walter railing about the end of the world — Noah and the Flood — in San Francisco's Union Square, complete with ambient noise and the wingbeats of a pigeon taking off. The phrase "it's gonna rain," delivered in Walter's dynamic, pitched lilt, sits exactly on the border between speech and song — which is what made it musically explosive material. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It's_Gonna_Rain), [2](https://www.nonesuch.com/journal/watch-steve-reich-composing-its-gonna-rain-2023-08-17)]

### 🔁 The accident of phasing

Reich played identical loops on two Wollensak tape recorders, intending to hold them in a fixed relationship. But 1965 consumer machines couldn't stay in sync: one tape crept gradually ahead of the other. Rather than fight the drift, Reich let it run — the two loops slide from unison through every possible offset, spawning new rhythms, melodies and quasi-harmonies before realigning. "Actually the going out of phase was kind of an accident," he told NPR, "but when I heard it I thought, 'This is fantastic.'" [[1](https://www.npr.org/sections/deceptivecadence/2015/01/27/381575433/fifty-years-of-steve-reichs-its-gonna-rain), [2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It's_Gonna_Rain)]

### 🎧 Two halves, light and dark

The piece falls into two roughly equal parts. Part I loops the title phrase itself, bright and almost dance-like as the voices chase each other. Part II takes different fragments of the sermon — the world after the Flood — and processes them with additional layering and tape delay into something far darker: intelligible warning disintegrating into an apocalyptic roar. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It's_Gonna_Rain)]

### 🧬 Legacy

Phasing became Reich's signature process: applied again to speech in *Come Out* (1966), then transferred to live instruments in *Piano Phase* (1967) and beyond. The idea that a gradual, audible process could *be* the composition underpins his manifesto "Music as a Gradual Process" and much of minimalism after it; Brian Eno has cited hearing It's Gonna Rain as his own gateway to minimalism and ambient music. Fifty years on it still sounds less like a study than a thunderclap.
