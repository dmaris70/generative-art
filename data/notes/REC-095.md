Graffiti Composition began in summer 1996, when Christian Marclay — commissioned by Berlin's Akademie der Künste for the Sonambiente sound-art festival — pasted more than 5,000 posters of blank music staff paper across the city's walls, hoardings and doorways. The public did the composing: passers-by scribbled notes, drawings, slogans and tags onto the empty staves, and Marclay photographed the results as they accumulated, weathered and were torn [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graffiti_Composition), [2](https://www.printedmatter.org/catalog/66348)]. It is one of the purest cases of a score generated by chance, vandalism and weather rather than a composer's hand.

### 🎼 The Score

From the photographic harvest Marclay selected 150 images and issued them as a portfolio of prints — the "score" of Graffiti Composition — intended for free interpretation by performers [[1](https://www.printedmatter.org/catalog/66348), [2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graffiti_Composition)]. The notation runs from earnest melodic fragments to obscenities, rips and rain damage; every mark, musical or not, is potentially playable. Like Marclay's turntable work, it treats the urban surface as a recording medium: the city inscribes itself, and musicians play it back.

### 🎸 The 2006 Performance

In 2006 Elliott Sharp was invited to realise the piece at The Museum of Modern Art, New York. He selected pages from the portfolio and led an ensemble of five guitarists — himself, Melvin Gibbs, Mary Halvorson, Lee Ranaldo and Vernon Reid — spanning no wave, free jazz, Sonic Youth and Living Colour lineages [[1](https://christianmarclay1.bandcamp.com/album/graffiti-composition), [2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graffiti_Composition)]. The one-off concert moved between notated fragments, conducted textures and open improvisation, the graffiti functioning as both instruction and provocation.

### 🔊 The Record

That MoMA performance, produced by Sharp, was released on 17 August 2010 by the Dog W/A Bone label [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graffiti_Composition), [2](https://www.discogs.com/release/2686155-Christian-Marclay-Graffiti-Composition)]. Reviewers heard in it the full range the score invites — from delicate hocketing to massed electric noise [[3](https://www.popmatters.com/137409-christian-marclay-graffiti-composition-2496074974.html), [4](https://disquiet.com/2010/07/08/christian-marclay-graffiti-composition/)].

### 🧬 Why It Matters

Graffiti Composition inverts the usual direction of graphic notation: instead of a composer inventing new symbols for performers, an anonymous public defaces the oldest symbol system in Western music, and performers must decide what counts as a note. It sits squarely in Marclay's larger project — later extended in works like his video scores — of making the visual residue of music audible again.
