Rhythms (composed 1980, released 1981 as Lovely Music LP 1301) is a suite of ten short duets between a computer-driven digital synthesizer and the percussionist Jan Williams. It is one of the first records to document what its composer, Joel Chadabe, would that same year name "interactive composing" — a way of making music in which the machine is not a playback device but a responsive partner. [[1](https://continuo.wordpress.com/2012/01/05/joel-chadabe-rhythms-2/), [2](https://direct.mit.edu/leon/article/55/1/107/108577/The-Emergence-of-Interactive-Music-The-Vision-and)]

### ⚡ A Synclavier Without a Keyboard

The equipment was a portable minicomputer/digital-synthesizer system built expressly for music by New England Digital Corporation of Norwich, Vermont — an early [Synclavier](https://continuo.wordpress.com/2012/01/05/joel-chadabe-rhythms-2/), pointedly ordered without its keyboard controller. Chadabe did not want to play the machine like an organ; he wanted to steer a running program.

* **The program:** built on an original rhythm structure whose parameters the composer could modify while it played.
* **The interaction:** the system could respond to live acoustic input from Williams's percussion in real time, so neither partner fully controlled the outcome. [[1](https://continuo.wordpress.com/2012/01/05/joel-chadabe-rhythms-2/)]

### 🥁 The Duo with Jan Williams

Williams — a longtime new-music percussionist and Chadabe's colleague — plays vibraphone, marimba, log and slit drums, woodblocks, temple blocks, cowbells and glass bowls against the computer's sequences. The ten tracks (42:22 total) carry deliberately light titles: Rendezvous, Bagatelle, Hot Sauce, After the Blues, Bird Bath, Carnival, Song Without Words, Yum Yum, Pas de Deux, Au Revoir. The synthetic timbres tend toward glass- and gamelan-like tones, and the rhythm programming nods to Caribbean and African patterns — genial surfaces over a radical method. [[1](https://continuo.wordpress.com/2012/01/05/joel-chadabe-rhythms-2/)]

### 🔁 Interactive Composing

Chadabe had been performing with self-regulating electronic systems since the early 1970s; Rhythms distilled the idea that a composer could design a process, then perform *with* it, "flying" the music by adjusting variables as the program made its own decisions. He formalized the term interactive composing in 1981, and the concept became the seed of an entire field — from live electronics to today's generative performance systems. [[1](https://direct.mit.edu/leon/article/55/1/107/108577/The-Emergence-of-Interactive-Music-The-Vision-and), [2](https://cdm.link/rip-joel-chadabe-a-leader-of-electronic-music-interactive-musicianship-and-teaching/)]

### 🧬 Afterlife

Rhythms was Chadabe's third LP and his first for [Lovely Music](http://www.lovely.com/bios/chadabe.html), the label of the downtown New York experimental scene. Chadabe (1938–2021) went on to found the Electronic Music Foundation and to write the standard history of the field, Electric Sound; obituaries singled him out as a pioneer who insisted that computers belonged in the concert moment, not just the studio.
