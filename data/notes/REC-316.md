Music on a Long Thin Wire is Alvin Lucier's celebrated sounding-sculpture: a single taut wire stretched across a room, driven by an oscillator through a magnet's field until the wire itself sings. Conceived in 1977 and released by Lovely Music in 1980 as a double LP (VR 1011/12), it distills Lucier's lifelong subject — the physical behaviour of sound in space — into one radically simple apparatus. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_On_A_Long_Thin_Wire), [2](http://www.lovely.com/albumnotes/notes1011.html)]

### ⚡ The apparatus

The construction is spelled out in the score-like description:

* A wire is extended across a large room and clamped to tables at both ends.
* Its ends are wired to the loudspeaker terminals of a power amplifier; a sine-wave oscillator feeds the amplifier, and a large horseshoe magnet straddles the wire at one end.
* The current flowing through the wire, interacting with the magnetic field, sets the wire vibrating; pickups at either end capture the vibration, amplified and broadcast in stereo. [[1](http://www.lovely.com/albumnotes/notes1011.html), [2](https://alvinlucierlovely.bandcamp.com/album/music-on-a-long-thin-wire)]

The catalogue note of an 80-foot wire reflects installation practice — the score fixes no length, and documented versions vary (Lovely's own description mentions a 50-foot span; installations have run far longer).

### 🔬 Origins in a physics class

The idea arrived in 1977 while Lucier and physicist John Trefny were teaching a musical acoustics course at Wesleyan University: a modern Pythagorean monochord — short wire, lab table, electromagnet, oscillator — turned out to produce startlingly complex sound [[1](http://www.lovely.com/albumnotes/notes1011.html)]. Lucier scaled the demonstration up into an environment.

### 🔊 What it sounds like

Nothing like the expected low drone. The Lovely recording sets the wire vibrating four times at four different frequencies, and the unstable system yields "a complexity of evocative, ethereal chords" — slides, beating patterns, sudden mode-jumps and choir-like harmonics as temperature, air currents and the wire's own metallurgy nudge it between states [[1](http://www.lovely.com/titles/cd1011.html)]. In installation the piece runs indefinitely, ideally with no one adjusting anything: after early performed versions, Lucier concluded the wire played itself better than any performer could.

### 🧬 Legacy

Alongside I Am Sitting in a Room (1969), the wire defined Lucier's poetics of letting physical phenomena be the music, and it became a founding work of sound-art installation practice — endlessly restaged in galleries since [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_On_A_Long_Thin_Wire), [2](https://theincubator.live/2017/03/05/alvin-lucier-music-on-a-long-thin-wire-1980/)]. Its lesson — set up honest conditions, then get out of the way — echoes through Di Scipio's ecosystems, Ellen Fullman's Long String Instrument and every artist who composes with physics rather than notes.
