In C, composed by Terry Riley in March 1964 and recorded for Columbia Masterworks in 1968, is the founding document of musical minimalism: a single page of 53 short melodic modules that any number of musicians play in order, each repeating each module as long as they like. Critic Robert Palmer called it "the single most influential post-1960 composition by an American," and the 1968 LP was added to the Library of Congress National Recording Registry in 2022. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_C), [2](https://www.yourclassical.org/story/2024/11/03/npr-in-c-forever-the-eternal-evolution-of-terry-rileys-minimalist-masterpiece)]

### 🎼 One page, 53 cells

The score specifies no instrumentation, no tempo and no fixed duration. The rules are social rather than syntactic:

* All players move through the 53 modules in sequence, at a shared pulse, each choosing when to advance.
* Players should stay within two or three modules of one another, listening across the ensemble rather than counting.
* An optional steady eighth-note pulse — high Cs on piano — keeps the group locked together.
* Riley's instruction: "Don't be in a hurry to move from figure to figure... listen for how it is relating to what the rest of the ensemble is playing." [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_C)]

The result is a shimmering, phasing cloud in which composed material and emergent texture are inseparable — a guided improvisation with a "carefully planned trajectory" from C-major patterns through modal shadings and back.

### 🤝 Premiere

The first performance took place on 4 November 1964 at the San Francisco Tape Music Center, with an ensemble that included Steve Reich and Pauline Oliveros; Reich suggested the eighth-note pulse that became standard practice [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_C), [2](https://www.kqed.org/arts/13967712/in-c-terry-riley-minimalism-music-history-maya-beiser)].

### 📀 The 1968 Columbia recording

Produced by composer David Behrman at CBS's 30th Street Studio in Manhattan on 27–28 March 1968, the recording used eleven musicians, from soprano saxophone to bassoon — and, over Columbia's initial resistance, Riley overdubbed three complete takes to thicken the texture, a pop-studio technique imported into concert music. The gatefold reproduced the entire score, effectively distributing In C as freeware and seeding decades of performances [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_C)]. Musicologist Robert Carl calls the LP the work's "second premiere": it stayed in Columbia's catalogue and placed minimalism in public consciousness [[1](https://www.tpr.org/arts-culture/2025-08-21/like-being-carried-away-down-a-sparkling-river-terry-rileys-classic-recordings-collected-in-new-box-set)].

### 🧬 Legacy

In C made process music joyful and communal where the postwar avant-garde had been forbidding, and its open instrumentation has invited realizations by gamelan ensembles, Chinese traditional orchestras, Africa Express and countless others — a piece that behaves less like a score than a protocol [[1](https://www.yourclassical.org/story/2024/11/03/npr-in-c-forever-the-eternal-evolution-of-terry-rileys-minimalist-masterpiece)].
