Mongolia: Traditional Music was issued in 1991 as CD D 8207 in the UNESCO Collection of Traditional Music (Auvidis/UNESCO, Paris), and reissued digitally by Smithsonian Folkways in 2014 when the Smithsonian assumed stewardship of the series [[1](https://folkways.si.edu/mongolia-traditional-music/world/music/album/smithsonian), [2](https://music.apple.com/us/album/mongolia-traditional-music-unesco-collection-from-smithsonian/893728361)]. Its thirty tracks (about 66 minutes) form a compact survey of Mongolian music at the moment the country emerged from the socialist era — field-style documentation of traditions since celebrated by UNESCO itself.

### 🗣 Khöömii: One Body, Two Voices

The disc documents khöömii (throat- or overtone singing), in which a single singer produces a low fundamental drone and simultaneously isolates its harmonics into a whistling melody above. Western Mongolia's Altai region is the tradition's heartland, and the practice has since been the subject of dedicated anthologies and UNESCO safeguarding attention [[1](https://routesnomades.fr/en/anthology)].

### 🎻 Horse Fiddle and Long Song

Two traditions on this recording were later proclaimed UNESCO Masterpieces of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity, and both anchor the programme [[1](https://folkways.si.edu/mongolia-traditional-music/world/music/album/smithsonian)]:

* The morin khuur, the two-stringed "horse-head fiddle", the emblematic Mongolian instrument whose repertoire imitates equine gaits and whinnies [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morin_khuur)].
* Urtyn duu, the "long song", in which a few words of text are extended over vast, ornamented melodic arcs — herders' music scaled to the steppe.

### 🎧 The Wider Programme

Beyond these headline genres the disc includes bogino duu (short songs) and pieces for metal and bamboo jew's harps, flutes, fiddles, lutes and zithers [[1](https://folkways.si.edu/mongolia-traditional-music/world/music/album/smithsonian)] — a deliberate breadth typical of the UNESCO series, which aimed at representative national surveys rather than single-genre portraits.

### 🕌 The Series

The UNESCO Collection, begun under Alain Daniélou in the 1960s and continued on Auvidis, is one of ethnomusicology's foundational catalogues; Smithsonian Folkways' 2014 relaunch returned over a hundred such albums to circulation with their original notes [[1](https://folkways.si.edu/mongolia-traditional-music/world/music/album/smithsonian)].
