Poppy Nogood and the Phantom Band (1968–69) is Terry Riley's soprano-saxophone drone masterpiece, released as the B-side of *A Rainbow in Curved Air* (Columbia/CBS, recorded 1968, released 1969). "Poppy Nogood" was Riley's alter ego at his legendary all-night concerts; the "Phantom Band" is not a band at all but the accumulating tape-delay ghost of Riley himself. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Rainbow_in_Curved_Air), [2](https://brahms.ircam.fr/en/works/work/33173/)]

### 🔁 The Time-Lag Accumulator

The piece is scored, in effect, for soprano saxophone, electronic keyboard and Riley's signature apparatus: two tape recorders sharing a single reel of tape, the second machine replaying what the first records after a delay of several seconds, the output fed back to be recorded again. [[1](https://brahms.ircam.fr/en/works/work/33173/)]

* Each phrase Riley plays is reinjected many times through the loudspeakers, overlapping with itself in progressively denser kaleidoscopic textures. [[1](https://brahms.ircam.fr/en/works/work/33173/)]
* Long modal saxophone lines — Riley had studied with the raga tradition in mind — pile into a phantom choir over a static drone, punctuated on the record by abrupt jump cuts between textures. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Rainbow_in_Curved_Air)]

### 🌙 All-Night Flights

The studio version distils Riley's "All-Night Flight" concerts of the late 1960s, at which he played from evening into morning — often over eight hours — to audiences who brought blankets and slept inside the sound. A tape of one such marathon, at SUNY Buffalo on 22 March 1968, has since been issued as *Poppy Nogood and the Phantom Band "All Night Flight."* [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Rainbow_in_Curved_Air), [2](https://boomkat.com/products/poppy-nogood-and-the-phantom-band-all-night-flight-suny-buffalo-new-york-22-march-1968-a1202b10-b57b-4f47-b109-c454a2c14e55)]

### ⚡ In the Studio

The album was among the earliest professional eight-track productions at CBS, letting Riley overdub every part himself — organ, electric harpsichords, dumbec and saxophone across the two sides — a one-man ensemble a decade before home multitracking. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Rainbow_in_Curved_Air)]

### 🧬 Legacy

The record carried minimalist process technique into rock culture: Pete Townshend credited it in the organ textures of "Baba O'Riley" (named partly for Riley) and "Won't Get Fooled Again," the band Curved Air took their name from it, and Riley's time-lag feedback system is the direct ancestor of every looper pedal and ambient delay practice since — Fripp and Eno's Frippertronics included. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Rainbow_in_Curved_Air)]
