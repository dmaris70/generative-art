A Rainbow in Curved Air, released in October 1969 on Columbia Masterworks (MS 7315), is Terry Riley's studio-made vision of ecstatic keyboard music — one musician, overdubbed into a shimmering orchestra [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Rainbow_in_Curved_Air), [2](https://www.forcedexposure.com/Catalog/riley-terry-a-rainbow-in-curved-air-lp/P.749408HLP.html)]. Where In C (1964) had made minimalism a social process for ensemble, Rainbow made it a psychedelic solo art, and in doing so seeded ambient music, prog and electronica.

### 🎹 One Man, Many Hands

On the eighteen-minute title track Riley plays everything himself by sequential overdubbing: electric organ, two electric harpsichords (a Baldwin electric harpsichord and an RMI Rock-Si-Chord), dumbec and tambourine [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Rainbow_in_Curved_Air)]. Parts were layered and synchronized in real time, without digital editing — a dense, interlocking lattice of pulse patterns, modal runs and long-breathed drones assembled from repeated studio passes [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Rainbow_in_Curved_Air)].

### 🔁 Improvisation as System

The music is largely improvised over modal scales, drawing on Riley's jazz background and his deepening study of Hindustani classical music [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Rainbow_in_Curved_Air)]. Repetition supplies the structure; improvisation supplies the content. The B-side, "Poppy Nogood and the Phantom Band", extends the method to soprano saxophone and organ run through Riley's tape-delay feedback setup, the sound of his all-night concert practice of the era [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Rainbow_in_Curved_Air)].

### 🌈 The 1969 Moment

Columbia Masterworks marketed the record to the rock generation as much as to new-music audiences, and the gatefold's utopian liner text ("...and then all wars ended...") made it a countercultural object. It remains the moment American minimalism crossed from loft concerts into mass-market psychedelia.

### 🧬 Influence

The album's fingerprints are everywhere: on ambient, new age, jazz fusion, and electronic music generally, and most famously on The Who's "Baba O'Riley", whose title honors Riley and whose burbling organ intro is a direct homage [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Rainbow_in_Curved_Air)]. For a catalogue of systems music, Rainbow matters as the proof that a repeating-pattern system could be voluptuous — rigor worn as radiance rather than as grid.
