Come Out (1966) is a thirteen-minute tape piece by Steve Reich, built from five recorded words spoken by nineteen-year-old Daniel Hamm of the Harlem Six [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Come_Out_(Reich))]. It is at once a foundational work of minimalist process music and a civil-rights document: the two facts are inseparable, and the piece cannot be honestly described without the case that produced the voice.

### ⚖️ The Harlem Six

The Harlem Six were six Black youths arrested for the 1964 murder of shopkeeper Margit Sugar in the weeks after Harlem's "Little Fruit Stand Riot" [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Come_Out_(Reich))]. Hamm, present at the riot but not responsible for the murder, was beaten by police; to be taken for medical treatment he had to prove he was injured. On tape he explains: "I had to, like, open the bruise up, and let some of the bruise blood come out to show them" [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Come_Out_(Reich))]. Civil-rights activist Truman Nelson asked Reich to edit voice recordings for a benefit supporting the Six's retrial; Come Out emerged from that commission [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Come_Out_(Reich))].

### 🔁 The Phasing Process

Reich loops the fragment "come out to show them" on two channels that begin in unison and slowly slip out of sync — the phasing technique he had discovered in It's Gonna Rain (1965) [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Come_Out_(Reich))].

* The drift first produces echo and reverberation-like doubling.
* The two voices then split into four, and four into eight, until speech dissolves into pulsing rhythmic and melodic texture.
* Nothing is added: every sound in the piece is Hamm's voice, transformed only by repetition and time.

### 🎧 Speech Becomes Music

The piece demonstrates Reich's core insight of the period — that recorded speech carries melody and that a gradual, audible process can be the entire composition. The words' meaning never fully disappears; the bruise stays in the room while the process abstracts it.

### 🧬 Legacy and Debate

Come Out became a cornerstone of minimalism and of politically engaged tape music, and its afterlife includes visual art (Glenn Ligon's Come Out paintings respond to it directly [[1](http://artjournal.collegeart.org/?p=13202)]). Recent scholarship has also interrogated the ethics of a white composer building a career-making work from a Black man's testimony of police violence [[1](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/currentmusicology/article/view/5221)] — a debate the piece now carries with it, appropriately, wherever it is heard.
