Schlingen-Blängen is Charlemagne Palestine's monumental work of continuous church-organ drone: a single unbroken sound-mass, sustained and slowly recolored for over an hour. The definitive recording — 70 minutes, made in 1988 in a small Dutch church near the North Sea — was issued by New World Records in 1999 (catalogue 80578), a decade after it was taped and roughly two after the piece's first performances. [[1](https://www.newworldrecords.org/products/charlemagne-palestine-schlingen-blangen), [2](https://www.soundohm.com/product/schlingen-blangen-2)]

### 🔊 One chord, held forever

The mechanism is disarmingly simple: Palestine wedges the organ's keys down — cardboard shims between the keys hold clusters sounding indefinitely — so the instrument becomes a self-sustaining drone machine. The performance then happens at the stops: by adding and subtracting registration, Palestine steers the standing sound through slow tidal changes of timbre, combination tones and beating partials, "pulling unbelievable sounds, colors and effects out of a familiar instrument". The organ, uniquely, can hold a chord as long as its blower runs — the piece simply accepts that invitation completely. [[1](https://www.soundohm.com/product/schlingen-blangen-2), [2](https://www.newworldrecords.org/products/charlemagne-palestine-schlingen-blangen)]

### 🕍 Sacred space as instrument

New World's notes frame the work as exploring "the physicality of sound as well as its spiritual dimensions": the church's acoustics are not a venue but a component, the building resonating like the body of the instrument. This is the sacred-ritual strain of Palestine's minimalism — the artist of Bösendorfer "strumming music" and cognac-and-teddy-bear ceremonies, translated to the loudest sustaining instrument in Western tradition. [[1](https://www.newworldrecords.org/products/charlemagne-palestine-schlingen-blangen), [2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne_Palestine)]

### 🎙️ The recording — what is and isn't certain

The 1988 tape was mixed for release by the composer Ingram Marshall. The published documentation identifies the location only as a small Dutch church near the North Sea and the piece as first performed about ten years before the recording (i.e., late 1970s); the sources consulted do not name the exact church or a precise premiere date, so this catalogue's "1988" should be read as the recording date, not the work's origin. [[1](https://www.soundohm.com/product/schlingen-blangen-2), [2](https://justinpatrickmoore.substack.com/p/new-world-records-and-the-american-2d3)]

### 🧬 Legacy

A seminal figure of early minimalism alongside La Monte Young and Terry Riley, Palestine directly influenced the massed-guitar composers Rhys Chatham and Glenn Branca; Schlingen-Blängen is his purest statement of duration-as-form, and the New World disc became the reference recording through which later drone and ambient musicians encountered it. [[1](https://www.newworldrecords.org/products/charlemagne-palestine-schlingen-blangen)]
