Dolmen Music (1981) is Meredith Monk's first album for ECM — catalogued as ECM New Series 1197 and the record that introduced her extended-voice ensemble music to a wide audience. Recorded in two sessions, at Hometown studio in New York (March 1980) and Tonstudio Bauer in Ludwigsburg (January 1981), and produced by Collin Walcott with Manfred Eicher, it was the first of twelve Monk releases on the label. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolmen_Music), [2](https://ecmrecords.com/product/meredith-monk-the-recordings-various/)]

### 🎤 The Voice as Instrument

Monk composes for the voice the way others compose for orchestra: wordless syllables, keening, ululation, glottal clicks and layered hocket, mostly over spare piano ostinati. Side one is intimate — "Gotham Lullaby" and "Travelling" for voice and piano, the miniature "The Tale," and "Biography" — Monk's solo vocabulary in concentrated form. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolmen_Music)]

### 🗿 The Title Suite

The 23-minute *Dolmen Music* (1979) fills side two: a six-voice ceremony for three women and three men with cello and percussion, its title evoking megalithic stone chambers — music that sounds simultaneously archaic and futuristic.

* **Ensemble:** Monk, Andrea Goodman, Monica Solem, Paul Langland, Robert Een (voice and cello) and Julius Eastman (voice and percussion) — Eastman's presence linking it to another key strand of downtown New York music. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolmen_Music)]
* The suite unfolds as overlapping modal cells, drones and invented ritual language, closing with Eastman's growling "Conclusion."

### 🏆 Reception

The album won Germany's Preis der deutschen Schallplattenkritik; Robert Christgau graded it A−, NME placed it among 1981's best albums, and NPR later ranked it at 147 in its "150 Greatest Albums Made by Women." [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolmen_Music)]

### 🧬 Legacy

For ECM the record was a threshold: an uncategorizable composer-performer on a jazz label, pointing directly toward the New Series imprint under which it is now catalogued, and toward Monk's later ECM landmarks (*Turtle Dreams*, *Book of Days*, *Atlas*). [[1](https://ecmreviews.com/2010/03/30/dolmen-music/)] Its afterlife crossed genres entirely: DJ Shadow sampled two of its tracks on "Midnight in a Perfect World" (1996), carrying Monk's voice into hip-hop's canon. Above all it proved that the "extended vocal technique" avant-garde could produce music of immediate, emotional beauty. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolmen_Music)]
