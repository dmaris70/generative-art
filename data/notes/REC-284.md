Music in Twelve Parts (1971–74) is Philip Glass's four-hour summa: a deliberate, encyclopedic compendium of every repetition technique he had evolved since the mid-1960s, written for the Philip Glass Ensemble and premiered complete at New York's Town Hall on 1 June 1974 — a hall Glass rented himself, and nearly filled. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_in_Twelve_Parts), [2](https://daily.bandcamp.com/lists/philip-glass-music-in-twelve-parts-guide), [3](https://classicalvoiceamerica.org/2024/05/30/revisiting-a-watershed-glass-12-parts-given-50-year-concert-revival/)]

### 🔁 An Accidental Opus

Only one piece was written at first, titled "Music in Twelve Parts" because it had twelve contrapuntal lines. When Glass played it for a friend, she asked what the other eleven parts would sound like. He liked the misunderstanding enough to compose them — eleven more Parts over three years, each a study in a different device: additive process (figures growing by one note at a time), cyclic structure (wheels of different lengths turning against each other), and, late in the cycle, rhythmic modulation and functional harmony creeping back in. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_in_Twelve_Parts), [2](https://daily.bandcamp.com/lists/philip-glass-music-in-twelve-parts-guide)]

### 🎹 The Ensemble Machine

The scoring is the classic Glass Ensemble: three electric organs (one held down continuously through the whole work), two flutes, four saxophones from soprano to tenor, and a wordless female voice riding the texture — ten instruments handled by a handful of hyper-drilled players plus sound mixer. Amplification is structural, not incidental: the band was built to project this music at rock volume with machine precision. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_in_Twelve_Parts), [2](https://philipglass.com/recordings/twelve_parts/)]

### 🎧 Stasis That Isn't

Tom Johnson, reviewing for the Village Voice in 1974, and every serious listener since, noted the central illusion: the music seems static, yet changes constantly — gradual, almost subliminal shifts that dissolve the stasis the moment you attend closely. Hearing all twelve Parts in sequence is a durational experience closer to meditation or endurance sport than to a concert, which is exactly the point. [[1](https://editions75.com/tvonm/articles/1974/philip-glass-in-twelve-parts.html), [2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_in_Twelve_Parts)]

### 🧬 Closing a Chapter

Part 12 famously introduces a twelve-tone row — Glass's wry salute to the serialist orthodoxy he had abandoned, planted at the end of minimalism's most exhaustive monument. Einstein on the Beach followed almost immediately; Twelve Parts thus stands as the hinge between process music and the operas. Three complete recordings exist (Virgin, Nonesuch 1993, Orange Mountain 2007), and the Ensemble revived the full cycle for its 50th anniversary in 2024.
