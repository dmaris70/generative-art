Mobile for Shakespeare (1960) is a thirteen-minute work by Roman Haubenstock-Ramati for soprano or mezzo-soprano and six players, setting texts by Shakespeare; it is published by Universal Edition, Vienna [[1](https://www.universaledition.com/en/Works/Mobile-for-Shakespeare/P0026886)]. It is the composer's signature demonstration of "mobile" form — a score whose sections, like the elements of a Calder sculpture, rearrange themselves differently in every performance. Some details of this work are uncertain (see below).

### 🎨 Calder in Sound

Haubenstock-Ramati (1919–1994), Kraków-born and later the leading experimentalist of postwar Vienna, took Alexander Calder's mobiles as a structural model: musical elements set "in constant motion with and against each other", producing the sound equivalent of the sculpture's constantly self-rearranging internal movements [[1](https://www.furious.com/perfect/haubenstock.html)]. The order of events shifts from performance to performance while the material itself stays fixed — form as suspension, not as trajectory.

### 🎼 Notation as Graphic Art

A pioneer of graphic notation (and organizer of its first major exhibition), Haubenstock-Ramati devised scores that define fields of possibility rather than fixed sequences [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Haubenstock-Ramati)]. For works of this kind he evolved an imaginative modern particella: the right-hand page outlines the musical action for the conductor, while the left-hand page carries the instrumental and vocal detail [[1](https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/haubenstock-ramati-roman)]. Performers realize pitch, rhythm, dynamics and ordering within visually specified constraints — the score is a machine for producing versions.

### 🔊 The Ensemble

The six players centre on resonant, decaying timbres — piano, celesta, vibraphone and three percussionists [[1](https://www.universaledition.com/en/Works/Mobile-for-Shakespeare/P0026886)] — a glittering, gamelan-like halo around the voice that suits music built from floating, recombinable moments rather than argued lines.

### 🧬 Legacy

With Mobile for Shakespeare and its companions, Haubenstock-Ramati established the mobile as a viable musical form and helped legitimize the graphic score as an object of study and exhibition; his influence runs through Cardew, the Polish avant-garde, and every composer who has since drawn a score rather than written one [[1](https://www.furious.com/perfect/haubenstock.html), [2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Haubenstock-Ramati)].
