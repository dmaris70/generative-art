This entry documents a performance practice rather than a single fixed work: the mid-2000s live-coding performances of Austrian composer Alberto de Campo, built on SuperCollider's pattern language — a vocabulary of stochastic event-generators that can be written, altered and re-evaluated while the music is running. De Campo, later Professor for Generative Art at the Universität der Künste Berlin, was among the musicians who made "the code as the interface" a performable proposition [[1](https://udk-berlin.academia.edu/AlbertodeCampo), [2](https://www.nkprojekt.de/supercollider-presentation-by-alberto-de-campo/)].

### ⚡ Patterns as Instrument

SuperCollider's patterns are compact descriptions of unfolding event streams — weighted random choices, Markov-like sequences, nested distributions — which the language renders into sound in real time. Performing with them means editing probability structures on stage: the performer steers tendencies, not notes. De Campo contributed key infrastructure to JITLib ("just-in-time library"), SuperCollider's framework for rewriting running programs, including its History classes, which record every line of code executed in a performance [[1](https://doc.sccode.org/Overviews/JITLib.html), [2](https://github.com/adcxyz)].

### 🔁 Conversational Programming

With Julian Rohrhuber, de Campo articulated the aesthetics of this practice in the paper "Improvising Formalisation: Conversational Programming and Live Coding" — programming reconceived as a dialogue with a running process rather than the preparation of a finished artefact [[1](https://supercollider.github.io/book/2009/09/12/article-on-conversational-programming-and-live-coding-in-supercollider)]. He has also written and taught extensively on live coding pedagogy in SuperCollider [[2](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/303808625_Live_coding_and_teaching_SuperCollider)].

### 🤝 PowerBooks UnPlugged

The practice's most distinctive vehicle was PowerBooks_UnPlugged, a six-member networked ensemble — de Campo, Rohrhuber, Hannes Hoelzl, Renate Wieser, Echo Ho and Jan-Kees van Kampen — who sat dispersed among the audience, shared code over a wireless network, and played only through their laptops' built-in speakers, styling themselves "the first acoustic computer music folk band" [[1](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/263128931_Herding_Cats_Observing_Live_Coding_in_the_Wild)]. Code written by one player could sound from another's machine, dissolving the link between gesture and source.

### 🧾 What Exists on Record

This catalogue entry is flagged uncertain, and rightly so. No canonical commercial recording titled to de Campo's 2005 pattern performances surfaced in research; the scene's early documents are scattered — conference papers, the TOPLAP organisation's compilation CDs of early live coding [[1](https://toplap.org/wiki/TOPLAP_CDs)], and code archives — rather than albums. The year 2005 is best read as marking a period of activity (SuperCollider live coding was then consolidating around JITLib and TOPLAP) rather than a datable release; the practice is well documented, its discography is not.
