This recording preserves a wasla — the classical Arab suite of vocal and instrumental pieces unified by a single melodic mode (maqam) — sung by Sabri Moudallal (1918–2006) and his Aleppo ensemble, captured in concert on 15–17 April 1988 at the Maison des Cultures du Monde in Paris [[1](https://www.mediatheque.be/collection/wasla-d-alep-chants-traditionnels-de-syrie-my6940), [2](https://www.discogs.com/master/1376572-Sabri-Moudallal-Et-Son-Ensemble-Wasla-DAlep-Chants-Traditionnels-De-Syrie)]. The muwashshah "Qultu lamma" is one of the suite's centrepieces [[3](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bm67L8V45ow)]. A label note: this catalogue credits Ocora, but the sources consulted tie the 1988 recording to the Maison des Cultures du Monde's own concert-recording series; the attribution should be treated as unconfirmed.

### 🕌 The Singer

Moudallal was for decades a muezzin at the Great Mosque of Aleppo, and among the most revered custodians of the city's classical repertoire [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabri_Moudallal), [2](https://www.pointculture.be/mediatheque/musiques-du-monde/wasla-d-alep-chants-traditionnels-de-syrie-my6940)]. His art sits exactly where Aleppine music lives: between sacred cantillation and secular courtly song, the same voice serving the call to prayer and the love lyric.

### 🎼 The Wasla

The wasla form — a chained sequence moving from instrumental prelude through composed muwashshahat to improvised solo passages, all in one maqam with gradually intensifying rhythm — survives today essentially only in Aleppo [[1](http://www.arabosounds.com/en/songs-from-aleppo/), [2](https://www.mediatheque.be/collection/wasla-d-alep-chants-traditionnels-de-syrie-my6940)]. The city's ensembles are prized for a specific colour: dialectal Aleppine intonation, a distinctive rhythmic sense, and the interplay of solo voice with responding chorus.

### 🎧 The Muwashshah

The muwashshah is a strophic song form descended from medieval Andalusia, its texts in classical Arabic, its settings often in long asymmetrical rhythmic cycles that reward — and require — deep listening. "Qultu lamma" ("I said, when…") runs about seven minutes in this performance [[1](https://open.spotify.com/album/5WuzNE46r2lyoWXnqcodXO)], the choir carrying the composed melody while Moudallal's ornamented solo lines float above it.

### 🧬 Why It Matters

These Paris concerts belong to the wave of late-1980s documentation that fixed the Aleppo school on record while its masters were still singing; Moudallal later recorded with the Ensemble Al-Kindi, becoming the tradition's international face [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabri_Moudallal)]. Since the destruction visited on Aleppo after 2012, recordings like this one have changed status: from documents of a living practice to part of its insurance policy.
