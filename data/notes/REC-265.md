This recording — Bali: Gamelan Semar Pegulingan / Gamelan of the Love God — documents the Semar Pegulingan club of Teges Kanyinan, a hamlet of Pliatan, Bali, and was made in the village by ethnomusicologist Robert E. Brown, with program notes by Brown and Philip Yampolsky. Issued on Nonesuch's Explorer series, it was the first commercial recording of this rare court gamelan apart from three collector's-item 78s. [[1](https://www.discogs.com/release/2855931-Semar-Pegulingan-Club-Of-Teges-Kanyinan-Gamelan-Semar-Pegulingan-Gamelan-Of-The-Love-God), [2](https://search.library.wisc.edu/catalog/999938315702121)]

### 🕌 The love god's gamelan

Semar is the Hindu god of love; pegulingan derives from a word for lying down. The gamelan semar pegulingan was a palace ensemble, played in an inner courtyard near the royal sleeping chambers in the afternoons and evenings — music to accompany the king's repose. Its repertory derives from the courtly gambuh dance-drama, with the suling flute melodies transposed to the trompong, a row of small kettle-gongs played by a single leading musician, supported by genders, drums, timekeeping gongs and the deep colotomic gongs that mark the cycle. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamelan_semar_pegulingan), [2](https://www.songlines.co.uk/content/features/balinese-gamelan-a-complete-guide-to-a-unique-world-of-sound)]

### 🎼 Seven tones, five chosen

Unlike the five-tone gamelans that dominate Bali, the semar pegulingan tradition uses a seven-tone pelog tuning (saih pitu) from which different five-tone modes (patutan) are selected per piece — one reason its sound is prized as sweeter and more ethereal than the brilliant gong kebyar style that displaced court music in the twentieth century. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamelan_semar_pegulingan), [2](https://asia.si.edu/research/essays/yamin/)]

### 📼 A near-extinct tradition, twice preserved

By the 1930s the ensemble type survived in only a few villages; the Teges Kanyinan club maintained a repertory rooted in the old court tradition while absorbing the innovations of modern Balinese playing. The Nonesuch record is thus a document of revival as much as survival — a village club keeping palace music alive after the palaces themselves had faded. [[1](https://www.nonesuch.com/albums/bali-gamelan-semar-pegulingan-gamelan-love-god), [2](https://asia.si.edu/research/essays/yamin/)]

### 🗓 A note on the date

The catalogue's 1988 date needs a caveat: the recording was originally released in 1972 as Nonesuch LP H-72046, and later remastered for CD in the Explorer series. 1988 most likely refers to a reissue or acquisition rather than the recording itself; the underlying tapes are from Brown's early-1970s fieldwork.
