Patchwork (composed December 1974 – March 1976) is Laurie Spiegel's four-voice computer piece made on the GROOVE system at Bell Laboratories, released on her landmark 1980 LP The Expanding Universe (Philo Records). Against the arhythmic, "overly cognitive" contemporary music of the day, Spiegel used a room-sized computer to write something melodic, danceable and warm — an early proof that algorithmic music could groove. [[1](https://unseenworlds.com/blogs/linernotes/laurie-spiegel-the-expanding-universe-notes-by-laurie-spiegel), [2](https://continuo.wordpress.com/2008/04/25/laurie-spiegel-the-expanding-universe/)]

### ⚡ The GROOVE System

GROOVE — Generating Realtime Operations On Voltage-controlled Equipment — was the hybrid system built at Bell Labs by Max Mathews and F. R. Moore: a computer running FORTRAN programs in real time, controlling analog synthesis hardware.

* The performer interacted through a keyboard, drawing tablet, pushbuttons and knobs, with the computer driving the voltage-controlled equipment that actually made the sound. [[1](https://continuo.wordpress.com/2008/04/25/laurie-spiegel-the-expanding-universe/)]
* Spiegel wrote her own interactive program for the piece, turning the lab apparatus into a playable compositional instrument. [[1](https://unseenworlds.com/blogs/linernotes/laurie-spiegel-the-expanding-universe-notes-by-laurie-spiegel)]

### 🔁 Four Motives, Four Rhythms

The piece is a lattice of small interlocking patterns, closer to an isorhythmic motet than to synthesizer noodling.

* Keys switched between **4 melodic motives** and **4 rhythms** (four different sequences of four loudness levels); other keys toggled the classical transformations — retrograde, inversion, augmentation, diminution. [[1](https://unseenworlds.com/blogs/linernotes/laurie-spiegel-the-expanding-universe-notes-by-laurie-spiegel)]
* Knobs controlled reverb mix, low-pass filtering and envelope decay in real time.
* Unusually for Spiegel, each of the four voices was composed independently as pitch–amplitude pairs — a "multitrack-like method" — with the melodies drifting in and out of prominence as their rhythms fluctuate against one another. [[1](https://unseenworlds.com/blogs/linernotes/laurie-spiegel-the-expanding-universe-notes-by-laurie-spiegel), [2](https://continuo.wordpress.com/2008/04/25/laurie-spiegel-the-expanding-universe/)]

### 🎧 Sources and Spirit

Spiegel names two inspirations: the isorhythmic motet and the spirit of banjo music — she is a trained lutenist and banjo player — and describes writing the piece "against the way it often feels to get up in the morning." The result is a bright, patterned dance whose logic is audible without being austere. [[1](https://unseenworlds.com/blogs/linernotes/laurie-spiegel-the-expanding-universe-notes-by-laurie-spiegel)]

### 🧬 Afterlife

The Expanding Universe sank quietly in 1980 and was rediscovered via Unseen Worlds' expanded reissues (2012, 2019), which recast Spiegel as a central figure of early computer music — a Bell Labs insider who used institutional machines for humane, folk-inflected ends. [[1](https://unseenworlds.com/products/laurie-spiegel-the-expanding-universe), [2](https://www.discogs.com/release/340640-Laurie-Spiegel-The-Expanding-Universe)]
