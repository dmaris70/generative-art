This entry gathers the drawn-sound compositions Daphne Oram realized on her Oramics machine in the mid-1960s — above all Pulse Persephone (1965), made for the Treasures of the Commonwealth exhibition at the Royal Academy of Arts. Oram, who co-founded the BBC Radiophonic Workshop in 1958 and left within a year to build her own studio, spent that decade proving an idea she had nursed since the 1940s: that a composer should be able to *draw* sound directly, bypassing both orchestras and tape splicing. [[1](https://www.daphneoram.org/aboutoram/), [2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oramics)]

### ⚡ The Oramics Machine

Designed from 1957 and built at her converted oast-house studio ("Tower Folly") in Kent with Gulbenkian Foundation grants — one in 1962, a second of £1,000 in 1965 that completed the composition machine — Oramics was a room-filling apparatus: a table-like metal frame carrying ten synchronized strips of clear, sprocketed 35mm film. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oramics), [2](https://www.daphneoram.org/oramicsmachine/)]

* The composer painted or drew shapes onto the film strips.
* The strips ran over photocells; the drawn masks modulated light falling on them.
* Separate tracks controlled pitch, duration, vibrato, timbre and dynamics — a hand-drawn, machine-read score.

### ✏️ The Drawn Compositions

The first entirely drawn-sound study, Contrasts Essonic, was recorded in 1963; Pulse Persephone followed in 1965 as the machine reached working completeness. The surviving pieces are short, wiry and strange — glassy tones with hand-wobbled vibrato, unquantized rhythm, timbres that sound neither instrumental nor quite electronic. Most circulated only privately until Paradigm Discs' Oramics anthology assembled her studio work decades later. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oramics), [2](https://daphneoram.bandcamp.com/album/oramics)]

### 📻 Before and After the BBC

Oram (1925–2003) had lobbied the BBC for years to take electronic sound seriously; the Radiophonic Workshop she co-founded became legendary, but its brief was broadcast effects, not composition, and she resigned in 1959 to pursue Oramics independently — a costly act of artistic self-determination for a woman in 1950s British broadcasting, funded thereafter by commercial jingles and film work. [[1](https://www.daphneoram.org/aboutoram/), [2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daphne_Oram)]

### 🏛 Rediscovery

The machine itself languished for years in a barn in France before being recovered; from 28 July 2011 to 2015 it was the centerpiece of the Science Museum's Oramics to Electronica exhibition in London, and it now sits in the Science Museum Group collection. Oram is today routinely cited as a founding figure of British electronic music, her graphic-control interface read as a prophecy of the modern DAW's drawn automation lanes.
