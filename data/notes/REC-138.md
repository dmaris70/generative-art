Wind Chimes (1987) is an acousmatic work by the New Zealand-born, UK-based composer Denis Smalley, commissioned by the South Bank Centre, London, and premiered at the Queen Elizabeth Hall's Electric Weekend on 11 September 1987 [[1](https://electrocd.com/en/oeuvre/14134/denis-smalley/wind-chimes)]. It is the canonical demonstration piece for spectromorphology — Smalley's own theory of how sound-shapes unfold in time — coined in print just before the work was made [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectromorphology)].

### 🔊 From One Ceramic Object

The piece grows from recordings of a set of ceramic wind chimes, whose fired-clay resonance "is audible in its natural state near the beginning of the piece and is never far away" [[1](https://electrocd.com/en/oeuvre/14134/denis-smalley/wind-chimes)]. As the sound-families expand, Smalley folds in complementary materials:

* a bass drum and very high metallic Japanese wind chimes
* resonant metal bars and interior piano sounds
* a small amount of digital synthesis [[1](https://electrocd.com/en/oeuvre/14134/denis-smalley/wind-chimes)]

The drama lies in hearing recognizably physical gestures — struck, scraped, swung — dissolve into purely spectral motion and back.

### ⚡ Two Studios

The work was composed in the Electroacoustic Music Studio of the University of East Anglia in 1987, but its computer sound-transformations were carried out in 1986 on the digital system of Studio 123 at the Groupe de Recherches Musicales (GRM) in Paris [[1](https://electrocd.com/en/oeuvre/14134/denis-smalley/wind-chimes)] — placing it exactly at the meeting point of the British acousmatic school and the French institution that invented the tradition.

### 🎧 Spectromorphology in Practice

Smalley coined "spectromorphology" in 1986 to name the perceived shaping of sound spectra through time; his 1997 paper "Spectromorphology: explaining sound-shapes" became one of the most-cited texts in electroacoustic theory [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectromorphology), [2](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1017/S1355771897009059)]. Wind Chimes has served ever since as the theory's worked example — the subject of multiple published analyses, including cognitive-framework studies using it as a test case [[1](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/280561512_An_Analysis_of_Denis_Smalley's_Wind_Chimes_Some_Preliminary_Results), [2](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/267395121_A_Cognitive_Framework_for_the_Analysis_of_Acousmatic_Music_Analysing_Wind_Chimes_by_Denis_Smalley)].

### 🧬 Releases

Wind Chimes first appeared in 1990 on the Computer Music Currents 5 CD (Wergo), and is now most readily heard on Smalley's collection Impacts intérieurs on the Montréal label empreintes DIGITALes [[1](https://electrocd.com/en/oeuvre/14134/denis-smalley/wind-chimes), [2](https://electrocd.com/en/produit/imed_0409--impacts-interieurs-cd/pistes)] — the label that has done most to keep the acousmatic repertoire in print.
