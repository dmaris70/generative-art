Philomel (1964) is Milton Babbitt's best-known work: a serial monodrama for live soprano and a four-channel tape of synthesized sound and electronically transformed voice, commissioned by the Ford Foundation for the soprano Bethany Beardslee. It was realized at the Columbia-Princeton Electronic Music Center on the RCA Mark II Sound Synthesizer, and it remains the classic demonstration that a live performer and a machine-made tape could be composed as one fabric. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philomel_(Babbitt)), [2](https://www.perfectcircuit.com/signal/rca-mkii-synthesizer-history)]

### 🎼 Hollander's Text and the Myth

The Yale poet John Hollander wrote the libretto to Babbitt's specifications — solo soprano, with playback through at least four sets of speakers surrounding the hall. The text retells Ovid's Philomela: raped and muted by King Tereus, she flees through the forest and is transformed into a nightingale, regaining voice as song. The piece runs in three sections — the flight, an echo-verse dialogue with the creatures of the forest, and five closing arias in which Philomel reclaims her voice. Hollander's word-music puns ("feel a million filaments…Philomel") are built directly into the sound. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philomel_(Babbitt))]

### ⚡ The RCA Mark II

The tape was made in early 1964 on the room-sized RCA Mark II at Columbia-Princeton, programmed via punched paper rolls that gave Babbitt exact control of frequency, timbre, rhythm and dynamics — precision no human performer could supply. Beardslee pre-recorded vocal fragments that Babbitt processed and layered into the synthesized texture, so the live singer performs against distorted images of her own voice. [[1](https://www.perfectcircuit.com/signal/rca-mkii-synthesizer-history), [2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philomel_(Babbitt))]

### 🔊 Voice Against Voice

The drama is the seam between live and recorded sound: the tape voice slides between speech, scream and song while the live soprano answers it, the myth of losing and regaining a voice enacted by the technology itself. Babbitt, characteristically, treated the synthesizer not as a sound-effects box but as the only performer accurate enough for his serial rhythms — a point he made repeatedly in interviews. [[1](http://preparedguitar.blogspot.com/2016/05/an-interview-with-milton-babbitt-by_6.html)]

### 🧬 Premiere and Afterlife

Wendy Carlos, then a Columbia graduate student, helped run the tape machines at the 1964 premiere. Beardslee's recording of the work (issued 1971) was later selected for preservation, with an essay published by the Library of Congress's National Recording Preservation Board — an unusual canonization for so uncompromising a piece. Philomel is now standard repertoire for adventurous sopranos and a fixture of every history of electronic music.
