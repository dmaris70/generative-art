Ben Johnston's String Quartet No. 4, "Amazing Grace" (1973), is a single continuous movement of variations on the folk hymn, each variation moving into a more elaborate tuning universe [[1](https://thelistenersclub.com/2024/05/29/ben-johnstons-string-quartet-no-4-amazing-grace-the-kronos-quartet/)]. This catalogue entry is the Kepler Quartet's recording, released on New World Records (80637-2) in January 2006 as part of the first complete traversal of Johnston's quartets [[1](https://www.newworldrecords.org/products/ben-johnston-string-quartets-nos-2-3-4-9)].

### 🎼 A Journey Through Tuning

Johnston (1926–2019), a student of Harry Partch, was the great pioneer of extended just intonation for conventional instruments — pure intervals tuned as whole-number ratios, and intervals far smaller than the tempered half-step [[1](https://www.newworldrecords.org/products/ben-johnston-string-quartets-nos-2-3-4-9)].

* The quartet opens with the hymn harmonized in a scale built from stacks of pure 3:2 fifths — Pythagorean tuning, austere and archaic [[1](https://www.bostonglobe.com/arts/2016/01/18/ben-johnston-quartet-unconventional-tuning-makes-hymn-sing/OKqxvmMeL49Cn4YH3aV7zN/story.html)].
* Successive variations open out into increasingly complex just-intonation lattices, with higher-limit ratios producing chords of uncanny purity and melodic steps no piano can play.
* Because the tune is universally known, listeners hear the tuning itself as the drama: the hymn sounds "both unexpected and deeply familiar" [[1](https://www.bostonglobe.com/arts/2016/01/18/ben-johnston-quartet-unconventional-tuning-makes-hymn-sing/OKqxvmMeL49Cn4YH3aV7zN/story.html)].

### 🕊 Why the Hymn

The choice of "Amazing Grace" is strategic, not sentimental. Johnston wanted microtonality to communicate; anchoring radical intonation to a vernacular melody lets untrained ears follow the pitch experiment as a musical narrative. The Fourth became his most performed work and the standard entry point to his music.

### 🎻 The Kepler Project

The Milwaukee-based Kepler Quartet — Sharan Leventhal and Eric Segnitz, violins; Brek Renzelman, viola; Karl Lavine, cello — spent roughly fourteen years recording all ten Johnston quartets across three New World discs, an effort of near-scholarly tuning rehearsal completed for the composer's ninetieth year [[1](https://newmusicusa.org/nmbx/ben-johnston-celebrating-90-years-with-10-string-quartets/), [2](https://www.newworldrecords.org/products/ben-johnston-string-quartets-nos-2-3-4-9)]. This 2006 disc (Quartets 2, 3, 4 and 9) launched the cycle.

### 🧬 Afterlife

The Kronos Quartet had earlier carried "Amazing Grace" to wide audiences [[1](https://thelistenersclub.com/2024/05/29/ben-johnstons-string-quartet-no-4-amazing-grace-the-kronos-quartet/)], but the Kepler cycle fixed the reference text: precise realizations, vetted by Johnston himself, of music once considered unplayably exact. The Fourth now stands beside Partch's instruments as American microtonality's most persuasive argument.
