Sardaigne: Launeddas (Ocora, 1984) presents two masters of Sardinia's ancient triple pipe — Luigi Lai and Aurelio Porcu — in recordings made in 1981–82 by ethnomusicologist Bernard Lortat-Jacob for Radio France. Issued in Ocora's authoritative field-recording series, the LP is a definitive document of one of Europe's oldest continuous instrumental traditions, captured from its finest living exponents. [[1](https://www.discogs.com/release/3598781-Luigi-Lai-Aurelio-Porcu-Sardaigne-Launeddas), [2](https://www.worldcat.org/title/sardaigne-launeddas/oclc/835786067)]

### 🎶 The instrument

The launeddas is three cane pipes played at once, each with an idioglot single reed (cut from the cane itself): a long drone pipe (*su tumbu*) and two melody pipes — one bound to the drone with wax, one free in the other hand — sounding in parallel thirds and sixths over the sustained bass. One player is thus a self-contained polyphonic ensemble: drone, harmony and two-part counterpoint from a single mouth. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Launeddas)]

### 🌬 Circular breathing

All three pipes sound continuously, without pause, by circular breathing — inhaling through the nose while the cheeks press stored air through the reeds. Masters sustain performances that can exceed an hour, built as chains of variations (*pikkiadas*) on core melodic phrases, an art of controlled escalation closer to a DJ set or a raga than to a European suite. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Launeddas)]

### 🏺 Antiquity

The tradition is genuinely archaic: a bronze statuette of the Nuragic civilization depicting a triple-pipe player attests the instrument on Sardinia since at least the eighth century BCE, with related multi-pipe reed instruments reaching back millennia further in North Africa and the Middle East. The launeddas accompanies both worlds of Sardinian communal life — religious processions and the round-dances (*su ballu*) danced in village squares. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Launeddas)]

### 🤝 The players

Luigi Lai (b. 1932, San Vito) is the heir of the Sarrabus school, trained in Villaputzu by Antonio Lara — who with his great rival Efisio Melis defined twentieth-century launeddas playing on 78s. Lai spent working years in Switzerland (studying at the Zurich Academy of Music) before leading the instrument's postwar revival, teaching widely and even reaching pop audiences with Angelo Branduardi in 1977. Aurelio Porcu, his elder colleague on the disc, represents the tradition's other lineage of village masters. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luigi_Lai), [2](https://www.discogs.com/Luigi-Lai-Aurelio-Porcu-Sardaigne-Launeddas/master/1277149)]

### 🧬 Why this record matters

Ocora's edition — made when the tradition had nearly died and was being rebuilt around figures like Lai — froze the repertoire at its moment of revival, and remains a touchstone for drone-minded listeners: continuous breath, beating reeds and hour-long variation form make the launeddas sound uncannily like a folk ancestor of minimalism.
