The ketjak — the Balinese "monkey chant," in which a hundred or more seated men become a percussion orchestra of interlocking voices — reached Western ears chiefly through David Lewiston's Nonesuch Explorer recordings. A dating note: the LP actually titled *Music from the Morning of the World* was recorded in 1966 and released in 1967, and contains only a brief ketjak excerpt; the complete performance, "Ketjak: The Ramayana Monkey Chant," fills the 1969 Nonesuch LP *Golden Rain* [[1](https://www.rootsworld.com/interview/lewiston.html), [2](https://www.discogs.com/release/1365477-Various-Golden-Rain-Balinese-Gamelan-Music-Ketjak-The-Ramayana-Monkey-Chant), [3](https://www.nonesuch.com/albums/bali-golden-rain)]. This entry's 1969 date points to the latter; the two records are best heard as one project.

### 🎧 The Recording

Lewiston, a British-born "musical tourist" who financed his first Bali trip himself, recorded, photographed and annotated these LPs for Nonesuch's pioneering Explorer series of world-music releases [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lewiston), [2](https://www.npr.org/sections/therecord/2017/06/05/531253921/david-lewiston-a-world-spanning-musical-tourist-who-brought-old-sounds-to-new-ea)]. *Music from the Morning of the World* — its title from Nehru's description of Bali — was inducted into the Library of Congress National Recording Registry in 2008 [[3](https://www.loc.gov/static/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/documents/MusicFromTheMorningOfTheWorld.pdf)].

### 🐒 The Chant

Ketjak stages the Ramayana: Prince Rama, aided by the monkey army of the Vanara, battles the demon king Ravana. The chorus chants "cak-cak-cak" in fast interlocking patterns — a vocal transposition of gamelan figuration — while soloists carry the narrative, arms flung skyward in unison [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayan_Limbak)].

### 🕌 Trance Roots, 1930s Invention

The form is younger than it sounds. The hypnotic chorus derives from sanghyang, a trance-exorcism ritual; in the 1930s the dancer Wayan Limbak (1897–2003) of Bedulu, working with German painter Walter Spies, grafted the Ramayana story onto the sanghyang chorus to create a free-standing spectacle — a collaboration candidly aimed at both Balinese tradition and Western visitors' tastes [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayan_Limbak), [2](https://www.academia.edu/23220285/The_Genesis_of_a_Dance_Genre_Walter_Spies_and_the_Kecak_in_Gottowik_ed_Die_Ethnographen_des_letzten_Paradieses_transcript_Bielefeld_2010)]. Limbak later toured the form worldwide.

### 🧬 Afterlife

The Nonesuch ketjak became a touchstone far outside ethnomusicology — a founding document for minimalists, samplers and film-makers drawn to massed, phasing human voices, and proof that a "traditional" masterpiece can be a modern co-invention that Bali then made entirely its own [[1](https://www.nonesuch.com/albums/bali-music-morning-world)].
