Black Origami (2017) is the second album by Jlin (Jerilynn Patton), released by the British label Planet Mu — first for streaming on 11 May 2017 and in physical formats on 19 May. It is the record on which a producer rooted in Chicago footwork stopped being explicable by footwork at all: a percussion music of ferocious precision that critics received as one of the decade's major electronic statements. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Origami), [2](https://jlin.bandcamp.com/album/black-origami)]

### 🌍 Gary, Indiana — footwork from the outside

Patton grew up in Gary, Indiana, about 25 miles from footwork's Chicago epicentre, and worked in the region's steel mills. Close enough to reach the clubs but choosing distance, she educated herself instead through YouTube videos of footwork dancers and bootleg recordings — an outsider's view that let her keep the genre's 160 BPM engine while discarding its samples and conventions. [[1](https://crackmagazine.net/article/album-reviews/jlin-black-origami/), [2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jlin)]

### 🥁 Rhythm as architecture

Where footwork tracks are typically built on chopped soul and rap samples, Black Origami is built almost entirely from drums: a wild battery of percussion — tablas, bells, military snares, voices used as struck objects — folded into constantly shifting metrical patterns, the "origami" of the title. Pitchfork scored it 8.8 and described it as a gorgeous, overwhelming piece of musical architecture; Resident Advisor and NPR heard the same thing — polyrhythmic tension that never resolves into a loop you can predict. [[1](https://ra.co/reviews/21055), [2](https://www.npr.org/2017/05/11/527658691/first-listen-jlin-black-origami), [3](https://www.nwitimes.com/entertainment/pitchfork-declares-gary-artist-jlin-had-one-of-years-best-albums/article_5ea480b6-24d2-58c0-94d8-b94685452767.html)]

### 🤝 Collaborators

The album's guest list maps how far Jlin's world had widened beyond the footwork circuit:

* **William Basinski** — the tape-loop composer sent over a loop of Baltic folk singers, which became the basis of "Holy Child". [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Origami)]
* **Holly Herndon** — fellow experimentalist, on "1%".
* **Dope Saint Jude** and **Fawkes** also appear. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Origami)]

### 🧬 Reception and afterlife

Black Origami received essentially universal acclaim and appeared on numerous year-end best-of lists, including Pitchfork's top ten albums of 2017 — striking coverage for a self-taught producer from Gary. It repositioned footwork as a compositional discipline rather than a regional dance style, and led on to Jlin's later collaborations with contemporary-classical performers.
