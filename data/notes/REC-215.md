Artikulation (1958) is a 3-minute-45-second electronic piece by György Ligeti, realized on magnetic tape at the Studio for Electronic Music of Westdeutscher Rundfunk (WDR) in Cologne — one of only three electronic works the composer ever completed. This recording pairs it with the object that made it famous to later generations: the Hörpartitur ("listening score") drawn by Rainer Wehinger and published in 1970. ### ⚡ Made in Cologne, 1958

Ligeti — recently escaped from Hungary — composed and notated the piece in January–February 1958, then prepared and recorded it on tape from February to March, assisted by Gottfried Michael Koenig and Cornelius Cardew. Conceived in quadraphonic sound, it was premiered on 25 March 1958 in WDR Cologne's "Musik der Zeit" concert series. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artikulation_(Ligeti))]

### 🗣 An artificial language

The title is literal: the piece behaves like speech. Short electronic sound-objects are grouped into "words" and "sentences" — chattering, questioning, interrupting, answering across the stereo field — a synthetic conversation with all the prosody of language and none of its meaning. It is the germ of the phonetic imagination Ligeti later unleashed in Aventures.

### 🎼 Wehinger's Hörpartitur

The piece's second life began over a decade later, when the graphic designer Rainer Wehinger created an "aural score" — published by Schott in 1970, with Ligeti's approval — as a post-compositional listening guide rather than a performance document:

* Each category of sound (impulses, noise, harmonic spectra, filtered material) gets its own colour and symbol family — combs, blobs, dots and wedges on a time grid.
* The score functions both as an introduction to how the work was constructed and as a perceptual map for the ear. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artikulation_(Ligeti)), [2](https://www.foldvaribooks.com/pages/books/980/gyorgy-ligeti-rainer-wehinger-erhard-karkoschka/aritikulation-elektronische-musik-eine-horpartitur-von-rainer-wehinger-electronic-music-an)]

### 🧬 The score that outshone the tape

Wehinger's graphics became a canonical example of notating the unnotatable — the first edition is now a collector's item, and a video syncing the score to the tape circulates widely online, introducing Artikulation to audiences far beyond electronic-music history seminars. For a catalogue of drawing systems, it is the exemplary case of a visual system built *after* the sound, to listen with.
