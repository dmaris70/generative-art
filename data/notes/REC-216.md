Five Piano Pieces for David Tudor (1959) is Sylvano Bussotti's notorious graphic-notation provocation — five extracts from his larger cycle *Pièces de chair II* (1958–60, a set of twenty-seven pieces), flung at the postwar avant-garde's greatest pianist. Written after Bussotti witnessed John Cage and David Tudor demonstrate indeterminate scores at Darmstadt in 1958 — an encounter that broke him loose from twelve-tone formalism — the pieces were premiered by Tudor at Darmstadt in 1959 to puzzled and contentious reactions, and published by Ricordi. [[1](https://www.getty.edu/publications/scores/03/commentary/), [2](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Piece-Four-Sylvano-Bussottis-Five-Piano-Pieces-for-David-Tudor-C-1959-Ricordi-Milano_fig1_287432396)]

### ✏️ A drawing becomes a score

The five pieces run the gamut from conventionally determinate notation (Nos. 2 and 5) to near-total indeterminacy (Nos. 3 and 4). For the infamous No. 4, Bussotti repurposed a curvilinear drawing he had made in 1949 — "a mixture of dots, regions, and squiggly lines" — and superimposed it on five musical staves, ink pooling among tangled lines that distort the staves themselves, with cryptic markings for mode of playing scattered through the cacophony. The visual object *is* the notation. [[1](https://www.getty.edu/publications/scores/03/commentary/), [2](https://www.getty.edu/publications/scores/object-index/154/)]

### 🤝 Tudor as decoder

Tudor did not improvise on the images; true to his practice, he translated them. His personal copy (now studied in the Getty's Scores Project) shows meticulous calculations converting graphics into customized performance scores — for No. 1 a tablature-like notation mapping finger placements across the keys. In performance he famously wore fingerless gloves to glide frictionlessly over the keyboard. The realizations blur composer and performer roles so thoroughly that scholars read the dedication as a kind of intimate collaboration conducted through notation. [[1](https://www.getty.edu/publications/scores/03/commentary/)]

### 🎹 Provocation and flesh

The parent cycle's title — "pieces of flesh" — signals Bussotti's program: an eroticized, theatrical, calligraphic music opposed equally to serial rigor and to Cageian ego-lessness. A 1960 Living Theatre performance in New York drew bemused press; at Darmstadt the work put graphic notation's central question — what exactly is being composed? — on the main stage of the European avant-garde. [[1](https://www.getty.edu/publications/scores/03/commentary/)]

### 🧬 An image with an afterlife

No. 4 became one of the most reproduced pages in twentieth-century music: printed in reviews as visual scandal, and ultimately placed by Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari at the opening of *Mille Plateaux* (1980) as an emblem of the rhizome. Few scores are better known by sight — and less often heard — a fate the Getty commentary treats as central to the work's meaning.
