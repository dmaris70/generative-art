Klavierstück XI (1956) is the most radical of Karlheinz Stockhausen's early piano pieces: nineteen notated fragments scattered across a single oversized sheet, to be played in whatever order the pianist's eye and hands decide, moment by moment. It is the emblematic European work of "mobile form" — determinate in every local detail, indeterminate in its overall shape — and a direct answer to the chance music then arriving from America. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klavierst%C3%BCcke_(Stockhausen)), [2](https://brahms.ircam.fr/works/work/12123/)]

### 🎲 The rule of the page

* The performer begins with whichever of the 19 fragments the eye falls on first, then proceeds to any other, wandering the page as through a labyrinth. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klavierst%C3%BCcke_(Stockhausen))]
* Each fragment ends with markings for tempo, dynamics and mode of attack — and these apply not to the fragment they close but to whichever fragment comes next. The same notes are therefore transformed every time they are revisited.
* When any single fragment has been reached for the third time, the performance ends. A version may be long or short, and may leave some fragments unplayed. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klavierst%C3%BCcke_(Stockhausen)), [2](https://stockhausenspace.blogspot.com/2015/06/klavierstuck-xi.html)]

### 🎼 Serial bones, mobile flesh

The freedom sits on top of a rigorous serial plan. The pitch material has nothing to do with twelve-tone rows: pitches are derived from the proportions of the rhythms Stockhausen had already composed, so duration structure generates pitch structure. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klavierst%C3%BCcke_(Stockhausen))] The result is a two-level form — every note fixed, the route through them free.

### 🤝 Tudor and the premiere

The idea reportedly goes back to a 1955 conversation in Cologne with the American pianist David Tudor, in which Stockhausen floated the notion of a piece whose performer could decide where to go on the page. Tudor — the same pianist who had premiered Cage's Music of Changes — gave the first performance on 22 April 1957 in New York, and his working materials for the piece survive in the Getty archive. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klavierst%C3%BCcke_(Stockhausen)), [2](https://www.getty.edu/research/tools/guides_bibliographies/david_tudor/av/klavierstucke.html)]

### 🧬 Open form's flashpoint

Appearing within a few years of Cage's chance works and alongside Boulez's own experiments, Klavierstück XI became the reference point for the European argument over aleatory music: how much of a work's identity survives when its sequence is surrendered? Recordings answer differently every time — Ensemble Musikfabrik's label, for one, issues the piece as separately numbered "versions" of the same score. [[1](https://www.musikfabrik.eu/en/label/work/stockhausen-klavierstueck-xi-version-1/)]
