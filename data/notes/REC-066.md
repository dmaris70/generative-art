Music in Fifths (1969) is one of the strictest of Philip Glass's early process pieces, composed in June 1969 for the amplified keyboards-and-saxophones lineup of the Philip Glass Ensemble. A single melodic line, doubled throughout at the interval of a fifth, is stretched and contracted by purely additive rules — minimalism reduced to arithmetic, and a deliberate joke at the expense of his Paris counterpoint teacher, Nadia Boulanger. [[1](https://philipglass.com/recordings/two_pages/), [2](https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/AdditiveMinimalism.html)]

### 🔁 Additive process

The piece is a textbook demonstration of the "additive process" Glass developed after transcribing Ravi Shankar's film music in Paris.

* A short pattern of even semiquavers is stated and repeated; then one note is added (or later subtracted), and the new pattern repeats — over and over, for the length of the work. [[1](https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/AdditiveMinimalism.html)]
* Glass described the piece as being in "closed form": a predetermined structure that ends when the accumulating repetitions have filled it out completely. [[1](https://philipglass.com/recordings/two_pages/)]
* The listener hears the line lengthen like a growing organism, without ever hearing a seam.

### 🎼 The Boulanger joke

Glass studied harmony and counterpoint with Boulanger from 1964, and parallel fifths are the cardinal sin of the species counterpoint she drilled into him. Music in Fifths is written *entirely* in parallel fifths — two lines locked a fifth apart, racing up and down in rigid parallel motion. Glass has called it "a sort of teasing homage" to her. [[1](https://philipglass.com/recordings/two_pages/)] The forbidden interval becomes the whole texture: an error promoted to a system.

### 🎹 The Chatham Square recording

The canonical recording was made in June 1973 at Butterfly Productions, NYC, for Chatham Square Productions — the artist-run label that documented Glass's ensemble years before any major would touch this music. Glass plays electric organ, Jon Gibson and Dickie Landry soprano saxophones, with Kurt Munkacsi (the ensemble's long-time sound engineer) recording and mixing; the performance runs 23:19. [[1](https://philipglass.com/recordings/two_pages/)] The piece has stayed in repertoire — Bang on a Can's later recording paired it with Two Pages. [[1](https://www.soundohm.com/product/music-in-fifths-two-pages)]

### 🧬 Why it matters

Together with Two Pages, Contrary Motion and Music in Similar Motion (all 1969), Music in Fifths defines the austere core of Glass's minimalism — before harmony, before Einstein on the Beach — where the "system" *is* the piece: a rule for growth, an interval, amplification, and stamina.
