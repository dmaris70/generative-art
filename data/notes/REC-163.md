I of IV was made by Pauline Oliveros in July 1966 at the University of Toronto Electronic Music Studio: a real-time tape piece, performed live in the studio with no editing, built from oscillators tuned mostly above the range of hearing and a long tape-delay chain. Released in 1967 on CBS's Odyssey label alongside Steve Reich's Come Out and Richard Maxfield's Night Music, it put a 34-year-old West Coast experimentalist's most oceanic music into record shops nationwide. [[1](https://www.cafeoto.co.uk/shop/pauline-oliveros-electronic-music-1965-1966/), [2](https://www.squidco.com/r.cgi?p=10228)]

### 🔊 Combination Tones

The sound source is deliberately perverse: sine oscillators set *above* audibility, whose difference (combination) tones — including beat frequencies against the tape recorder's own bias frequency — fall back down into the audible range. What you hear is largely sound that no oscillator is actually producing: ghost tones generated by the interaction of ultrasonics with the recording equipment itself. Oliveros described this technique herself in interviews as the basis of I of IV and its siblings. [[1](https://tapeop.com/interviews/41/pauline-oliveros)]

### 🔁 The Tape-Delay Installation

In the Toronto studio Oliveros patched a large-scale installation: oscillators and filters into a mixer, then one spool of tape threaded through a series of stereo tape machines, creating long delay lines that fed back on themselves. The result is a slow avalanche — deep droning pile-ups of noise, sirening glissandi and rich sonorities accumulating over the piece's span — all shaped by hand, in the moment, as a performance rather than an assemblage. [[1](https://www.cafeoto.co.uk/shop/pauline-oliveros-electronic-music-1965-1966/), [2](https://imprec.bandcamp.com/track/ii-of-iv-university-of-toronto-electronic-music-studio-1966)]

### 📻 An Unlikely Hit

The Odyssey LP (New Sounds in Electronic Music) made I of IV one of the most widely heard electronic works of its decade; it remains her most famous tape piece. Companion works from the same Toronto summer (II of IV, and others) circulated only decades later, on Paradigm Discs' Electronic Works and Important Records' 12-CD Reverberations set — which is why I of IV long stood alone as the public face of this whole body of work. [[1](http://stalk.net/paradigm/releases/pd04.html), [2](https://imprec.bandcamp.com/album/pauline-oliveros-reverberations-tape-electronic-music-1960-1970-12cd)]

### 🧬 Toward Deep Listening

The piece's method — set up conditions, then listen and respond in real time — is continuous with everything Oliveros (1932–2016) did afterward: the Sonic Meditations, the ♀ Ensemble, and the Deep Listening practice that made her one of experimental music's most influential teachers. Alvin Lucier's memorial tribute places her, rightly, among the composers who redefined listening itself as the compositional act.
