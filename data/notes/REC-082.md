For 1, 2 or 3 People (1964, Edition Peters) is Christian Wolff's best-known score: a piece for one to three players on any instruments whatever, in which almost nothing is fixed except *relationships*. Wolff calls such works "contingent" — music that exists only in performers reacting to each other in real time. David Tudor, the pianist who premiered much of the New York School's music, made the work practically his own, and his realizations of it are among the most striking documents of 1960s experimentalism. [[1](https://www.wisemusicclassical.com/features/2024/12/christian-wolff-at-90/), [2](https://nwr-site-liner-notes.s3.amazonaws.com/80737.pdf)]

### 🎼 A notation of cues

The score is graphically notated as a network of relationships between performers: each musician listens intently for cues to begin or end sounds in relation to the others, so that one sound triggers others in a cascade of responsive events. Most events are specified only generally — "high in some aspect," "a sound in some way dissonant with what immediately precedes," "a harmonic," "a sound made by tapping or touching or tracing or the like." A solo performer may coordinate with sounds in the environment, or with "other notes given on a page … to provide something to coordinate with." [[1](https://nwr-site-liner-notes.s3.amazonaws.com/80737.pdf)]

### 🎹 Tudor's baroque-organ realization

In 1968 Tudor recorded the piece — remarkably — on sculptor Richard Lippold's **baroque organ**, in a session produced by David Behrman for CBS. He built a double-layered performance: one layer played at the keyboard, the other from techniques "analogous to the prepared piano" — removing pipes, partially opening and closing vents, playing the pipes percussively, and amplifying small sounds with contact microphones. Behrman recalled night-shift editing marathons at Columbia, with Tudor "very particular about what he wanted to use." [[1](https://nwr-site-liner-notes.s3.amazonaws.com/80737.pdf)]

### 🔁 The 1972 recording

The 1968 session's outtakes fed a 1970 live version made for the Merce Cunningham Dance Company's *Tread*; a 24-minute 1972 recording from a Cunningham tour — Tudor on baroque organ and live electronics — opens *The Art of David Tudor 1963–1992* (New World Records 80737-2), remixing "all the highly distinctive sounds of the 1968 LP … into a new and much longer realization." [[1](https://nwr-site-liner-notes.s3.amazonaws.com/80737.pdf)]

### ⚠️ What is uncertain here

The catalogue's "Tudor realization" flag is fair: 1964 is the composition date only, and Tudor's documented realizations date from 1968 (the Behrman-produced LP) and 1972 (the New World recording) — which one this entry's listening copy reflects is unresolved. Wolff himself, though, singled out Tudor's Columbia recording of the piece as exemplary: "His actions, performing, were absolutely clear and decisive, no matter how indeterminate the material."
