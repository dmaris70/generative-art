Synchronisms No. 6 (1970) is a roughly eight-minute work for piano and electronic sounds by the Argentine-American composer Mario Davidovsky, realized at the Columbia-Princeton Electronic Music Center in New York. It premiered at the Tanglewood Music Festival on 19 August 1970, played by Robert Miller of the Group for Contemporary Music, and won the 1971 Pulitzer Prize for Music — one of the first Pulitzers awarded to an electroacoustic work [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synchronisms_(Davidovsky)), [2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Davidovsky)].

### ⚡ Columbia-Princeton craft

Davidovsky settled in New York in 1960 and became one of the central figures of the Columbia-Princeton studio, splicing his tape parts by hand from oscillator tones [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Davidovsky), [2](https://music.columbia.edu/news/mario-davidovsky-1934-2019)]. The Synchronisms series pairs a live instrument or ensemble with a fixed tape part; No. 6, for the most public of solo instruments, became the series' emblem.

### 🎹 One coherent instrument

The piece's governing idea is that tape and piano should fuse into "a single, coherent musical and aesthetic space" [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synchronisms_(Davidovsky))]. The electronics behave pianistically — attacks that decay like struck strings — while the tape does what the piano cannot: it makes notes crescendo after the attack, bends sustain, and extends resonance. The famous opening gesture, a single piano tone taken over and swelled by the tape, announces the game immediately.

### 🎼 Selective synchronization

Davidovsky did not demand mechanical lockstep with the tape:

* Precise coordination is required "only in short passages of intricate counterpoint."
* In longer stretches one component simply accompanies the other, deliberately admitting an element of chance in the alignment [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synchronisms_(Davidovsky))].

This pragmatic solution to the live-plus-tape ensemble problem was widely imitated.

### 🏆 Pulitzer and reception

The 1971 Pulitzer jury wrote that the work "shows mastery of a new medium and its imaginative use in combination with the solo pianoforte" [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synchronisms_(Davidovsky)), [2](https://hearingthepulitzers.podbean.com/e/episode-29-1971-mario-davidovsky-synchronisms-no-6/)]. The prize legitimized tape music inside the American concert establishment at a moment when it was still widely regarded as laboratory curiosity.

### 🧬 Afterlife

Pianist Aleck Karis recorded the work on his 1996 album Secret Geometry, and violinist-composer Mari Kimura has cited the piece as the spark for her own move into electronic music [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synchronisms_(Davidovsky))]. It remains the most-performed of the twelve Synchronisms and a fixture of the piano-and-electronics repertoire — the piece by which generations of pianists first learned to play with a loudspeaker as chamber partner.
