This recording documents the sacred tanbur repertoire of the Ahl-e Haqq ("People of the Truth," also called Yarsan), a Kurdish esoteric religious order centered in the Gurān region of western Iran, for whom the long-necked lute tanbur is the exclusive sacred instrument of worship. An honest flag on the release itself: the catalogue's attribution to a 1995 Ocora disc could not be confirmed in available sources. The closest verified Ocora item is Jean During's double CD "Kurdistan: Zikr et chants soufis" (Ocora Radio France, 1994, C 560071/72), and Ocora's Kurdish-Iranian catalogue from these years is thinly documented online — the exact disc behind this entry remains uncertain. ### 🕌 The Yarsan and the Jam

The Ahl-e Haqq hold their liturgy in the jam (djamm), a devotional assembly in which sacred hymns (kalâm) are sung to the tanbur; the repertoire and its meanings are transmitted orally within families and lineages, and much of it was long kept from outsiders. Scholarship on the Gurān communities describes a precisely graded body of religious-musical knowledge, with modes tied to specific texts, saints and ritual moments. [[1](https://oraltradition.org/religious-musical-knowledge-and-modes-of-transmission-among-the-kurdish-ahl-e-haqq-of-guran/), [2](https://oraltradition.org/the-religious-textual-heritage-of-the-yarsan-ahl-e-haqq/)]

### 🪕 The Instrument

The Kurdish tanbur is deceptively modest: a pear-shaped mulberry-wood body and walnut neck, around fourteen gut frets, two or three steel strings (the highest usually doubled), with a vibrating length near 60 cm. It is treated as a sacred object — never placed on the ground — and played with a technique unique to the tradition: the shorr, a continuous tremolo produced by strumming with the whole right hand rather than plucking single strings, which gives the music its characteristic shimmering, wave-like surface. [[1](https://detoutetdorient.com/la-voie-celeste-du-tanbur-kurde/), [2](https://jambands.com/features/2004/12/31/journey-of-the-jam-kurdistan-s-sacred-tanbur/)]

### 🎼 The 72 Maqams

The sacred repertoire is organized as a corpus traditionally numbered at 72 maqams — modes-cum-melodies of graded sanctity. By the 1990s their complete mastery had narrowed to very few musicians; Ali Akbar Moradi (b. 1957, Gurān region), widely acknowledged as the tradition's greatest living virtuoso, undertook to record the full cycle precisely because he was among the last who knew it whole. The great mystic-musician Ostad Elahi (1895–1974) represents the tradition's other modern summit, his tanbur playing famed for its ecstatic force. [[1](https://jambands.com/features/2004/12/31/journey-of-the-jam-kurdistan-s-sacred-tanbur/), [2](https://detoutetdorient.com/la-voie-celeste-du-tanbur-kurde/)]

### 🧬 Why Recordings Matter

Because transmission is oral, endogamous and partly secret, the 1990s field recordings by During and others are not merely documents but preservation infrastructure for a minority faith under long-standing pressure in Iran — which is exactly what a state-radio ethnographic label like Ocora existed to do.
