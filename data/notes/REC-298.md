These are the acoustic-era 78-rpm recordings of Tanburi Cemil Bey (Istanbul, 1873 – 28 July 1916), the supreme instrumental virtuoso of late Ottoman classical music, made mostly between 1910 and 1914, before the electric microphone existed. Across roughly one hundred fifty discs he documented his playing on tanbur, kemençe, yaylı tanbur and lavta — a recorded legacy that still defines Turkish instrumental style more than a century later. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cemil_Bey), [2](http://www.turkishmusicportal.org/en/composers/detail/tanburi-cemil-bey)]

### 🕌 The taksim tradition

The heart of these records is the taksim: a solo, non-metric improvisation that unfolds and explores a makam (the Ottoman-Turkish modal system, cognate with the Arabic maqam), moving through its characteristic pitches, phrases and modulations before returning home. Cemil Bey is regarded as the genre's great transformer — his taksims turned a functional prelude into a self-sufficient art of intense concentration, and performers today still use techniques he developed and popularized. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cemil_Bey), [2](https://sites.google.com/mc.edu/copy-resonances/home/unit-3-music-for-entertainment/chapter-8-listening-at-home-and-at-court/tanburi-cemil-bey-sam%C3%A2i-shad-araban)]

### 🎻 One musician, many instruments

* Trained first on violin and kanun, he was famous among Istanbul's tanbur players by age twenty.
* On disc he ranges across the tanbur (the Ottoman long-necked lute), the small upright bowed kemençe, the bowed yaylı tanbur, and the lavta — an almost unheard-of breadth of mastery.
* His recordings also set the model for the saz semai instrumental suite form, and many of his compositions remain among the most performed in the repertory. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cemil_Bey), [2](http://www.turkishmusicportal.org/en/composers/detail/tanburi-cemil-bey)]

### 📻 Recording at the empire's end

The discs were cut acoustically — the musician playing into a horn — in the Ottoman capital in the years before World War I; Cemil Bey died of tuberculosis in 1916, aged 43, so the shellac horizon of 1910–14 is effectively all the sound of him that exists. The catalogue's 1910 date is best read as the approximate start of this recording window rather than a single session. [[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cemil_Bey)]

### 🧬 Afterlife

The 78s have been anthologized repeatedly, notably in multi-volume CD editions (e.g. the Traditional Crossroads volumes held by research libraries), and his taksims function as a canon: students transcribe them, and modern masters of kemençe and tanbur record homages to his repertoire. Few bodies of early recordings anywhere carry comparable authority within a living tradition. [[1](https://searchworks.stanford.edu/view/5990454), [2](http://goldenhorn.com/records/ghp026.html)]
