Ginastera's Violin Concerto, Op. 30, was commissioned by the New York Philharmonic and premiered on 3 October 1963 by Ruggiero Ricci with Leonard Bernstein conducting [[1](https://usuo.org/explore/2022/04/ginastera-violin-concerto/)]. It is the fullest orchestral statement of the Argentine composer's third, "neo-expressionist" period, in which the gauchesco nationalism of his early ballets gave way to twelve-tone rows, quarter-tones and violent instrumental virtuosity — TIME covered the premiere under the headline "On to Surrealism" [[1](https://content.time.com/time/magazine/article/0,9171,873778,00.html)].

### 🎻 A Premiere on No Rehearsal

Ginastera finished the score only in September 1963 — too late for its originally intended occasion — and Ricci and the orchestra did not rehearse the full concerto together until the morning of the premiere [[1](https://usuo.org/explore/2022/04/ginastera-violin-concerto/)]. The performance nevertheless succeeded: the players themselves rose shouting "bravo", for the absent composer as much as for Ricci's command of the solo part [[1](https://usuo.org/explore/2022/04/ginastera-violin-concerto/)].

### 🎼 An Inverted Architecture

The concerto's form is deliberately back-to-front:

* It opens with the solo cadenza, followed by a chain of études for the violinist — studies in thirds, other intervals, harmonics — making analysis precede argument.
* The slow movement, "Adagio per 22 solisti", dissolves the orchestra into twenty-two individual soloists surrounding the violin.
* The finale is a whispering perpetuum mobile scherzo, marked to be played at the edge of audibility, with the soloist in near-constant motion.

The writing is serial in pitch organization but remains dramatic, coloristic and unmistakably Ginasteran in rhythmic drive.

### ✨ Ricci and the Dedication

The work is bound to its first performers: commissioned by and dedicated to the New York Philharmonic, with Bernstein on the podium and Ricci — the Paganini specialist of his generation — as soloist [[1](https://usuo.org/explore/2022/04/ginastera-violin-concerto/)]. Ricci's papers, including material from this period, are held at the Library of Congress [[1](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/gdc/gdcfindingaidpdfs/mu017011/mu017011.pdf)].

### 🧬 A Slow Afterlife

Despite its spectacular premiere the concerto remained a rarity, kept alive in scattered performances and recordings. Its profile rose sharply when Hilary Hahn recorded it (with the Frankfurt Radio Symphony, for Deutsche Grammophon), a project publicized by Ginastera's publisher Boosey & Hawkes [[1](https://www.boosey.com/cr/news/Hilary-Hahn-Records-Ginastera-s-Violin-Concerto/102078)] — a belated mainstream vindication of one of the fiercest violin concertos of the 1960s.
